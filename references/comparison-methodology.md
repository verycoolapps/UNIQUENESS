# Comparison Methodology — Systematic Comparison Protocol

> How to structure a rigorous, unbiased, multi-dimensional comparison.

---

## 1. Establishing a Valid Comparison Baseline

Before any comparison begins, the baseline must be explicitly defined. An undefined baseline produces comparisons that look rigorous but prove nothing.

### The 5 Baseline Dimensions

**1. Subject Identity**
Confirm exactly what is being compared. Products compared at different lifecycle stages (v1 vs. v5) are not equivalent. A brand's current identity vs. its 2015 identity requires temporal qualification.

**2. Comparison Universe**
Who are the legitimate alternatives? Be specific:
- Global vs. regional vs. local scope
- Same category only, or adjacent categories?
- Same price tier, or cross-tier comparison?
- Same audience, or different audiences?
- Established players only, or include emerging alternatives?

The comparison universe must be realistic and fair. Comparing a local artisan bakery to a multinational food corporation is valid only if the goal is to find relative uniqueness — the frame must be stated clearly.

**3. Timeframe**
Is the comparison about current state, historical state, or trajectory? A company might be undifferentiated today but on a rapidly differentiating trajectory. State the timeframe explicitly.

**4. Audience or Market Segment**
The same product can be highly differentiated for one audience and completely generic for another. Define whose perspective the comparison is taken from.

**5. Evidence Scope**
What information is available? Define what sources are included and what their limitations are. A comparison based only on marketing materials will produce different findings than one based on user research, technical analysis, and market data.

---

## 2. The 7 Comparison Fallacies to Avoid

Comparative analysis is structurally vulnerable to biased reasoning. Identify and avoid these seven fallacies.

### Fallacy 1 — Cherry-Picking Dimensions
Selecting only the dimensions where Subject A excels and ignoring dimensions where Subject B is superior (or equivalent). Every comparison must include dimensions where each subject might have an advantage, even if that weakens the original hypothesis.

**Detection**: Ask — "Am I choosing these dimensions because they're relevant, or because they favor a predetermined conclusion?"

### Fallacy 2 — False Equivalence
Treating two subjects as comparable when they serve fundamentally different purposes, audiences, or contexts. A luxury sports car and an economy hatchback cannot be compared on the same value-for-money scale — they serve different functions for different buyers.

**Fix**: Define what "comparable" means precisely before proceeding. If the subjects are genuinely incomparable on some dimension, say so rather than forcing a parallel.

### Fallacy 3 — Moving Goalposts
Changing the comparison criteria mid-analysis when initial results don't support the expected conclusion. The comparison framework must be established at the outset and held consistently throughout.

### Fallacy 4 — Comparison Without Context
Declaring something "unique" or "better" without specifying compared to what, for whom, in what context, and according to what criteria. Example: "This product has superior design" — superior to what, according to whose criteria, for which users?

### Fallacy 5 — Present-State Bias
Treating the current state as the permanent state. A product that is currently undifferentiated may have patents in development, partnerships forming, or a roadmap that will create significant differentiation within 12 months.

**Fix**: Explicitly address trajectory and future state as a separate dimension.

### Fallacy 6 — Surface-to-Surface Comparison
Comparing only what is visible and stated, without investigating underlying structures. Two companies with identical feature lists may have radically different business models, cost structures, and sustainable advantages.

**Fix**: Always investigate Layer 2 (structural) and Layer 3 (identity) differences alongside Layer 1 (surface) differences.

### Fallacy 7 — Confidence Inflation
Presenting tentative findings as confident conclusions. If the evidence is thin, the confidence must be proportionally low. Never compensate for weak evidence with strong language.

## 3. Multi-Dimensional Comparison Methodology

### Phase 1 — Dimension Generation
Generate comparison dimensions specific to the domain and subjects. Do not use a fixed template. For each potential dimension, ask: *"Does this dimension actually differentiate meaningful value for the relevant audience?"*

Dimensions that answer "no" can be deprioritized. Dimensions that reveal unexpected differences should be highlighted.

### Phase 2 — Dimension Weighting
Not all dimensions carry equal weight. Apply weighting based on:
- **Audience priority**: Which dimensions matter most to the people who use, buy, or interact with the subject?
- **Strategic importance**: Which dimensions drive competitive advantage?
- **Evidence quality**: Dimensions with strong evidence should carry more weight than those with weak evidence
- **Stability**: Dimensions that are durable over time are more strategically important than volatile ones

Document the weighting rationale. Weights should be defensible, not arbitrary.

### Phase 3 — Evidence Mapping
For each dimension, map the available evidence to each subject. Use the evidence classification system:
- 🟢 Verified Fact
- 🔵 Observation
- 🟡 Inference
- 🟠 Hypothesis
- ⚪ Unknown

### Phase 4 — Cross-Dimensional Analysis
After individual dimension analysis, look for patterns across dimensions:
- Do multiple dimensions point to the same underlying structural difference?
- Are there tension points (where Subject A leads on one dimension but trails on a correlated dimension)?
- Do any findings seem inconsistent with other findings? Investigate why.

## 4. Similarity Analysis Protocol

Similarities are as analytically important as differences. They define the competitive baseline and reveal which differentiation claims are real vs. which are superficial variations on a shared foundation.

### Surface Similarity vs. Deep Similarity

**Surface similarity**: Two subjects share a visible characteristic (both use blue in their branding, both have a mobile app, both offer a free tier).

**Deep similarity**: Two subjects share an underlying structural or philosophical characteristic that shapes most of their observable behavior (both are built on a subscription + community flywheel; both treat design as a primary competitive moat rather than a cosmetic decision).

**Analysis instruction**: For every identified similarity, ask: *"Is this a surface similarity or a deep similarity?"* Deep similarities are strategically more significant than surface ones, because they indicate that the two subjects are genuinely competing on the same fundamental model — meaning true differentiation requires structural changes, not cosmetic ones.

### The Similarity-to-Differentiation Ratio

Calculate (qualitatively) how much of the subject's total identity is shared vs. distinctive.
- **High similarity ratio** (>70% shared): The subject is likely competing as a commodity or generic alternative. Only a small portion of its identity is genuinely differentiating.
- **Medium similarity ratio** (40-70% shared): Meaningful differentiation exists, but the subject still competes within established category conventions.
- **Low similarity ratio** (<40% shared): The subject has broken significantly from category conventions and may occupy a genuinely distinctive position.

## 5. Difference Analysis Protocol — 8 Types

Every identified difference should be classified by type. Different types of differences have different strategic implications.

| Type | Description | Strategic Significance | Example |
|------|-------------|----------------------|---------|
| **Functional** | Different capabilities — it can do things alternatives cannot, or cannot do things alternatives can. | High — creates direct use-case advantage | A project management tool that integrates with hardware sensors, which competitors lack |
| **Structural** | Different underlying architecture — built differently even if the output looks similar. | Very high — usually difficult to copy | A financial platform built on real-time streaming data vs. competitors built on batch processing |
| **Philosophical** | Different core beliefs that drive decision-making differently. | Highest — almost impossible to copy authentically | A company that refuses outside investment on principle, shaping every strategic decision |
| **Behavioral** | Acts differently in similar situations — different defaults, different responses to challenges, different communication patterns. | High — creates different experiences | A support team that proactively calls users when they detect struggling behavior, vs. waiting for tickets |
| **Experiential** | Creates a different subjective experience even with similar functional outputs. | Very high for consumer categories | Same food, but one restaurant creates anticipation, ceremony, and memory around the eating experience |
| **Strategic** | Different approach to market, competition, and growth — different theory of winning. | High for long-term analysis | A company that grows exclusively through word-of-mouth vs. paid acquisition in a category where paid acquisition is standard |
| **Emotional** | Generates different emotional responses in users, buyers, or observers. | High for brand and lifestyle categories | Products that create pride of ownership vs. products that are simply functional |
| **Temporal** | Leads or lags competitors on a time dimension — was first, or is building toward future differentiation others don't yet have. | Context-dependent | A company that invested in AI capabilities in 2021 when competitors waited until 2024 |

## 6. The Apples-to-Oranges Problem

When subjects are from fundamentally different categories, standard comparison breaks down. Apply this protocol.

### Step 1 — Identify the Legitimate Comparison Purpose
Why is this comparison being made? Usually one of:
- Understanding which best solves a specific problem for a specific user
- Understanding which approach/philosophy produces better outcomes
- Understanding whether Subject A could disrupt Subject B's market
- Understanding what Subject A could learn from Subject B's category

### Step 2 — Find the Shared Comparison Dimension
Even very different subjects can be compared on purpose-level dimensions:
- Both aim to solve a specific user problem → compare problem-solving effectiveness
- Both compete for a user's time/attention/budget → compare value delivered per unit of resource consumed
- Both represent an approach to a shared challenge → compare the approach's strengths and weaknesses

### Step 3 — Make the Frame Explicit
State clearly: *"This comparison is not evaluating these subjects on identical criteria. Instead, we are comparing [Subject A] and [Subject B] on the shared dimension of [specific purpose]."*

A fair apples-to-oranges comparison is entirely valid when the frame is stated honestly. The fallacy only occurs when the frame is hidden.
