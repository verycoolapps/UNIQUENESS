# Research Standards — Evidence Protocol & Intellectual Integrity

> The rules that separate rigorous analysis from confident-sounding speculation.

---

## 1. Source Hierarchy

Apply sources in order of reliability. When a lower-tier source contradicts a higher-tier source, the higher-tier source takes precedence — or the conflict must be explicitly flagged.

### Tier 1 — Primary Sources (Strongest)
Direct evidence from the subject itself or verified firsthand accounts.
- Official product documentation
- Published financial disclosures (annual reports, SEC filings, investor presentations)
- Registered patents and patent applications
- Published academic research by the organization's own researchers
- Direct statements from verified founders, executives, or creators (in official contexts)
- Official technical specifications
- Primary user research conducted by credible researchers
- Court records and regulatory filings

### Tier 2 — Secondary Sources (Strong)
Analysis and reporting by credible intermediaries who accessed primary sources.
- Reputable independent journalism (established publications with editorial standards)
- Independent academic research
- Industry analyst reports from established firms
- Verified case studies conducted by credible third parties
- Expert professional commentary with disclosed methodology

### Tier 3 — Tertiary Sources (Moderate — verify before using)
Aggregated or derived information — useful for leads, requiring verification.
- Comparison websites (verify that data matches primary sources)
- Aggregator databases
- Wikipedia (useful for structure; verify claims against cited primary sources)
- Informal expert community discussions (must be corroborated)

### Tier 4 — Weak Sources (Use with explicit caution flag)
Promotional or unverified content.
- Marketing materials produced by the subject
- Self-reported metrics without independent verification
- Anonymous online reviews (useful for pattern-spotting, not factual claims)
- Social media content (unless from verified official accounts)

**Rule**: Any uniqueness claim based primarily on Tier 4 sources must be classified as 🟠 **HYPOTHESIS** unless independently corroborated.

## 2. Verifying Uniqueness Claims — Search Protocol

When a claim of significant uniqueness needs verification, apply this protocol:

### Step 1 — Define the Verification Scope
Before searching, define: *What would constitute adequate verification?*
- Geographic scope: local, national, regional, global?
- Category scope: direct competitors only, or adjacent categories?
- Time scope: current state, or including historical alternatives?
- Audience scope: which users or buyers are relevant?

Write down the scope before searching. Do not expand or narrow it based on what the search finds.

### Step 2 — Search Systematically, Not Conveniently
Search for alternatives that *would falsify* the uniqueness claim, not for evidence that confirms it. Confirmation bias is the primary threat to honest uniqueness analysis.

For each claimed unique characteristic, ask: *"What would I expect to find if this characteristic were NOT unique?"* Then specifically look for those alternatives.

### Step 3 — Document What Was and Wasn't Searched
In the final analysis, disclose:
- What sources were examined
- What search terms were used (if applicable)
- What was NOT searched and why
- What information would change the conclusion

### Step 4 — Apply the Evidence Classification
After searching, classify the finding:
- 🟢 **VERIFIED FACT**: Found in Tier 1 or Tier 2 sources, directly confirming or refuting the claim
- 🔵 **OBSERVATION**: Directly observable from available material without research
- 🟡 **INFERENCE**: Reasonably derived from multiple consistent indicators, without direct confirmation
- 🟠 **HYPOTHESIS**: Plausible given available evidence, but requires testing or deeper research
- ⚪ **UNKNOWN**: Insufficient evidence to make any reliable classification

## 3. The "Unprecedented" Verification Protocol

The most dangerous claim in uniqueness analysis is "first," "only," or "unprecedented." These claims are almost always stated before sufficient evidence exists. Apply this strict protocol.

### Requirements Before Claiming "Unprecedented"

**Minimum requirements**:
1. Define the exact claim: *"First/only [specific characteristic] for [specific use case] among [specific audience] in [specific geography] as of [specific date]"*
2. Search major alternatives in the defined scope (at least the top 10–15 competitors if applicable)
3. Examine adjacent categories that could serve the same need
4. Search historical records within the timeframe
5. Classify the resulting finding as:
   - **"Verified as unprecedented within [scope] as of [date]"** (Tier 1–2 evidence found confirming absence)
   - **"Potentially unprecedented within [scope] based on available research"** (searched but not exhaustively; no alternatives found)
   - **"Uncommon within [scope]"** (exists, but rare)
   - **"Unverified"** (insufficient search performed)

**What NEVER qualifies as adequate verification**:
- Not finding something in a quick search is not proof it doesn't exist
- Marketing claims by the subject itself
- Peer opinions without evidence
- The absence of search results on a single platform

**Standard language for strong but unexhausted search**:
> "Within the [defined scope], available research did not identify an alternative offering [specific characteristic]. This suggests the characteristic is [rare / potentially unprecedented within this scope], though a comprehensive global audit was not conducted and exceptions may exist."

## 4. Citing Evidence in Analysis

Every major factual claim must have an associated evidence classification marker. Format:

```
[Claim statement] [Evidence marker] [Source or basis]
```

**Examples**:
- "The company operates on a revenue-share model rather than subscriptions." 🟢 Source: Official pricing page, accessed [date]
- "The design language appears to deliberately reference Bauhaus principles." 🔵 Observation from examining the product's visual system
- "The team's academic background likely influences their research-first methodology." 🟡 Inference from published team bios + product development blog posts
- "The company may have more than 50% female users, based on the skewed social media audience." 🟠 Hypothesis — requires demographic data to confirm
- "Revenue figures for this private company are not publicly available." ⚪ Unknown — cannot assess economic differentiation without this data

## 5. Handling Conflicting Evidence

Conflicting evidence is common in real-world research. Handle it with this protocol:

1. **State the conflict explicitly**: Do not suppress one side of the evidence to maintain a clean narrative
2. **Assess source quality**: Higher-tier sources take precedence
3. **Assess recency**: More recent evidence generally supersedes older evidence (unless historical context is the point)
4. **Assess specificity**: More specific evidence (a detailed technical specification) generally supersedes general evidence (a marketing claim)
5. **If genuinely unresolved**: Classify the finding as 🟠 **HYPOTHESIS** and state both sides of the evidence conflict

**Example of handling conflict**:
> "The company claims their AI achieves 97% accuracy on benchmark X (Tier 4 — marketing material). An independent researcher's audit found 89% accuracy on a similar test (Tier 2). These figures conflict; the independent audit is considered more reliable, but the methodological differences between tests are unknown. Finding classified as 🟡 INFERENCE: accuracy is competitive with industry alternatives, but the company's highest claims require independent verification."

## 6. Research Scope Definition

What constitutes "the market," "the category," or "the world"?

### "The Market"
The set of alternatives a buyer would seriously consider when making the same type of decision. This is narrower than the category and broader than direct competitors. Example: for a premium organic coffee subscription, "the market" includes other premium coffee subscriptions, high-end local roasters, and specialty grocery options — not all coffee products globally.

### "The Category"
All products or services that serve the same fundamental need, regardless of positioning. Example: the coffee category includes instant, pod, ground, whole bean, cold brew concentrate, coffee shops, subscription services, and vending machines.

### "The World"
Genuinely global scope requires genuinely global research. Claiming global uniqueness based on English-language searches misses Japanese, Korean, German, Brazilian, Indonesian, and other market leaders. When a truly global search is not feasible, state this limitation clearly.

## 7. Anti-Fabrication Rules — Absolute Prohibitions

These rules are non-negotiable:

1. **Never invent competitor characteristics**: If you do not know what a competitor does, say so. Do not infer beyond the evidence.
2. **Never invent market size or share data**: Do not estimate revenue, users, or market penetration without a credible source.
3. **Never invent user research findings**: Do not claim "users prefer X" without actual user research data.
4. **Never simulate having searched sources that were not actually accessed**: If a tool was unavailable, disclose that the analysis is limited.
5. **Never assign specific dates to events without a source**: "The company launched in 2019" requires a source — do not approximate from context clues and present it as fact.
6. **Never quote people**: Do not attribute statements to real people unless quoting from verified, cited sources.
7. **Never infer private company financials from public signals**: Revenue, valuation, and employee count for private companies cannot be reliably estimated without disclosed data.

**When you don't know**: Say exactly this — "This information was not available in the research conducted. The analysis is limited on this dimension." That is always better than fabricating.

## 8. Researching Different Subject Types

| Subject Type | Primary Evidence Sources | Common Limitations | Key Uniqueness Indicators |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Public companies** | SEC/regulatory filings, earnings calls, patents, official documentation | Investor-facing language may exaggerate; competitive intel may be intentionally vague | Business model, IP portfolio, financial structure, market positioning |
| **Private companies** | Founder interviews, product, job postings (reveal internal priorities), customer reviews, press coverage | Minimal financial disclosure; claims are often unverified | Product differentiation, team background, operational signals from job postings |
| **Creative works** | The work itself, creator statements, critical analysis, historical context | Subjective dimensions require acknowledged subjectivity | Originality of concept, style signature, cultural context, compositional choices |
| **Individual people** | Portfolio, public work, stated credentials, verifiable achievements | Privacy limits; credentials require verification | Skill combination, cross-domain synthesis, demonstrated outcomes |
| **Products** | Official specifications, independent reviews, lab tests, teardowns | Marketing specs may differ from real-world performance | Technical architecture, materials, manufacturing decisions, interaction design |
| **Scientific claims** | Peer-reviewed publications, independent replications, patent filings | Findings may be preliminary; research is ongoing | Novelty relative to existing literature, methodological rigor, applicability |
| **Restaurants/Experiences** | Menu, chef background, independent reviews, observation | Subjective experience varies; reviews may be biased | Concept originality, sourcing, technique, experience design |
