# Scoring Methodology — Uniqueness Scoring System

> How to assign, interpret, calculate, and communicate uniqueness scores with intellectual integrity.

---

## 1. The 0–10 Scoring Scale — Concrete Anchors

Scores are **structured analytical judgments**, not objective measurements. Every score must be explained, not just stated. Use these anchors to calibrate consistently.

| Score | Label | Concrete Definition | Example |
|-------|-------|--------------------|----|
| **0** | No differentiation | The characteristic is identical to virtually all alternatives in the comparison universe. No meaningful variation exists. | A generic white-label SaaS with zero customization, resold as-is under a different name |
| **1** | Negligible | Technically different but the difference is invisible to users, buyers, or observers without detailed inspection. | A food brand with a slightly different shade of blue on its packaging vs. competitors |
| **2** | Minimal | A small observable difference that most audiences don't notice or don't value. Provides no measurable advantage. | A mobile app with a marginally cleaner onboarding flow than its category average |
| **3** | Slight | A real difference that some audiences will notice. Creates minor advantage in specific contexts, but not a primary decision driver. | A restaurant with slightly longer hours than its direct competitors |
| **4** | Moderate-Low | A meaningful difference noticed by informed buyers. Creates occasional competitive advantage. Not yet a distinctive identity element. | A consultancy with a proprietary diagnostic framework that clients find useful but which competitors could build in 6 months |
| **5** | Moderate | Clearly differentiating for a segment of the audience. Creates measurable advantage in relevant use cases. Present in a minority of competitors. | A fitness app with video analysis of form vs. text-only feedback alternatives |
| **6** | Moderate-High | A significant differentiator valued by the core audience. Present in very few competitors. Creates sustained advantage in head-to-head consideration. | A B2B tool with native data sovereignty features in a market where competitors route everything through US servers |
| **7** | Strong | Clearly distinctive. The audience reliably associates this characteristic with the subject. Difficult for competitors to replicate quickly. | A law firm with a named proprietary methodology for startup equity structuring, with published thought leadership and 15 years of case history |
| **8** | Very Strong | A defining characteristic that meaningfully separates the subject from virtually all alternatives in the defined scope. High defensibility. | A coffee roaster that has exclusive multi-year contracts with specific farms — competitors cannot access the same beans |
| **9** | Exceptional | An outstanding differentiator that creates category-level separation. The subject is reliably recognized as the reference point for this characteristic. | An authentication company whose core technology has 47 patents, deployed at a scale that makes it the de facto infrastructure standard |
| **10** | Unmistakable | The characteristic is so deeply embedded, so widely recognized, and so difficult to replicate that it has become a defining reference in the category. Almost impossible to confuse with any alternative. | Apple's industrial design language — not just different, but the standard against which industrial design decisions in adjacent categories are measured |

## 3. How to Avoid Grade Inflation

The most common scoring failure is inflation — where everything rates 7–9 because the analyst wants to deliver impressive findings. Inflation destroys the discriminating power of the system.

**Anti-inflation rules**:
1. **Anchor to the full scale**: Before scoring any dimension, recall the 0 and the 10 anchors. Most real-world scores should cluster between 3 and 7.
2. **Require justification for 8+**: Any score of 8 or higher requires explicit explanation: *what specific evidence justifies this rating? How many alternatives were examined and found lacking this characteristic?*
3. **Score relative to defined scope**: Scores are always relative to the defined comparison universe — not to some abstract ideal.
4. **Apply the peer check**: Ask — *"If a skeptical expert read this score, would they find the justification convincing?"*
5. **Expect the median**: In most honest analyses of real-world subjects, the weighted composite will fall between 4.5 and 6.5. Consistently high composites suggest inflation.

---

## 4. Absolute vs. Relative Scores

**Absolute scores** rate the subject against an idealized standard regardless of competition. Used when evaluating intrinsic quality or achievement.

**Relative scores** rate the subject against the defined comparison universe. Used when evaluating competitive differentiation.

UNIQUENESS uses **relative scoring by default** — scores are always interpreted as "within the defined comparison universe." State this clearly in every scorecard. If the comparison universe changes, the scores change.

**Example**: A local artisan ice cream shop may score 8/10 on Distinctiveness within its city's ice cream category, and 4/10 on Distinctiveness within the global premium dessert market. Both scores are correct. They just measure different things.

## 5. Composite Score Calculation

```
Composite = Σ (Dimension Score × Dimension Weight)
```

**Example calculation** (Business domain):

| Dimension | Score | Weight | Weighted Score |
|-----------|-------|--------|----------------|
| Distinctiveness | 7 | 20% | 1.40 |
| Value Creation | 6 | 18% | 1.08 |
| Defensibility | 8 | 12% | 0.96 |
| Rarity | 5 | 8% | 0.40 |
| Originality | 4 | 10% | 0.40 |
| Meaningfulness | 6 | 15% | 0.90 |
| Audience Relevance | 7 | 12% | 0.84 |
| Combination Strength | 5 | 5% | 0.25 |
| **TOTAL** | | **100%** | **6.23 / 10** |

**Interpretation of 6.23**: This subject is moderately-to-meaningfully differentiated within its comparison universe. It has real competitive advantages but is not yet in the "distinctive" or "rare" territory. Specific dimensions (Defensibility: 8, Distinctiveness: 7) represent genuine strengths to build from.

## 6. Expressing Confidence and Uncertainty in Scores

Every score should carry an explicit confidence level:

| Confidence | Meaning | Display |
|------------|---------|---------|
| **High** | Evidence is strong, well-sourced, and directly supports the score | Score: 7 ✓ |
| **Medium** | Evidence is reasonable but incomplete or partly inferred | Score: ~7 |
| **Low** | Significant information gaps — score is a rough estimate | Score: 6–8? |
| **Insufficient** | Evidence is too weak to score meaningfully | Score: N/A |

When confidence is Low or Insufficient, explicitly state what information would be needed to produce a confident score.

---

## 7. How to Present Scores to Users

**Transparency requirements**:
1. Always state the comparison universe before presenting scores
2. Always explain the dominant weight adjustments and why they were applied
3. For every dimension score, provide a 1–2 sentence rationale
4. Present the composite score with its confidence level
5. Explicitly note that scores are analytical judgments, not objective measurements
6. Identify which 2–3 dimensions most strongly drive the composite score

**What to avoid**:
- Presenting scores as if they came from a neutral algorithm
- Claiming false precision (never write 6.237 — round to one decimal place)
- Letting scores replace explanatory analysis — the score is a summary, not the analysis itself
- Implying that a high uniqueness score means commercial success or overall quality
