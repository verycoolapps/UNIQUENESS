# MULTI-COMPETITOR Workflow
## UNIQUENESS — Competitive Landscape Analysis (3–20 Subjects)

---

## ACTIVATION TRIGGERS

This workflow activates when:
- User provides 3 or more named subjects for comparison
- User asks "how does X compare to its competitors?" and multiple competitors are known
- User asks for a competitive landscape overview
- User asks which company/product/approach is most distinctive in a category
- User asks "where is the white space in [market]?"

**Minimum subjects for this workflow: 3**
**Maximum subjects for single analysis: 20** (beyond 20, recommend segmentation)

---

## ORGANIZING ANALYSIS AT SCALE

When many subjects are involved, the analysis can collapse into an overwhelming table. Prevent this with **progressive structure**:

1. **Cluster first** — group similar subjects before comparing individuals
2. **Map positions** — place subjects on the competitive landscape
3. **Find the standouts** — identify subjects that break from cluster norms
4. **Zoom in on differentiation** — deep analysis only for distinctive players
5. **Synthesize** — produce a readable, actionable summary

---

## CLUSTERING METHODOLOGY

### Step 1 — Initial Categorization
Sort subjects by their most dominant characteristic:
- Price tier (premium / mid-market / value)
- Audience focus (mass / niche / enterprise / consumer)
- Functional approach (how they deliver value)
- Design philosophy (how they present themselves)
- Business model (what they are built around)

### Step 2 — Cluster Assignment
Group subjects into 2–5 clusters. Name each cluster with a descriptor that captures the shared identity:

| Cluster Name       | Shared Characteristics       | Members              |
|--------------------|------------------------------|----------------------|
| "The Specialists"  | Narrow, deep, expert-focused | A, D, G              |
| "The Platforms"    | Broad, horizontal, integrations | B, C, F           |
| "The Disruptors"   | Price/model innovation       | E, H                 |

### Step 3 — Intra-Cluster Differentiation
Within each cluster, ask: **What makes each member different from others in the same cluster?** Cluster members that look identical externally often have deep hidden differences.

### Step 4 — Inter-Cluster Analysis
Compare clusters as units:
- Which cluster has the most members? (indicates crowded space)
- Which cluster has the fewest? (indicates underexplored positioning)
- Which cluster has the strongest combined differentiation?

---

## MAPPING COMPETITIVE POSITIONS

### Dimension Selection for Mapping
Choose 2–3 dimensions that best capture the competitive spread. Avoid dimensions where all subjects score similarly. Good mapping dimensions create visual spread.

**Examples:**
- Complexity vs. Accessibility
- Price vs. Feature depth
- Niche vs. Broad appeal
- Traditional vs. Innovative approach
- Automation vs. Human involvement

### Position Map (Text Format)
```
HIGH [Dimension Y]
│                    ● Subject C
│         ● Subject A
│                              ● Subject F
│    ● Subject E
│              ● Subject B          ● Subject D
│
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────
LOW [Dimension Y]           HIGH [Dimension X]
```

Annotate the map:
- **Dense zones** — overcrowded, high competition, low differentiation opportunity
- **Sparse zones** — potential white space (validate attractiveness separately)
- **Outliers** — subjects far from any cluster (most differentiated or most niche)

---

## FINDING LEAST-CROWDED POSITIONS

Scan the position map for coordinates with no or few subjects. For each sparse zone, evaluate:

1. **Is it sparse because it's unattractive?** (No demand, no viable business model, been tried and failed)
2. **Is it sparse because no one has thought to occupy it?** (Genuine opportunity)
3. **Is it sparse because it requires capabilities few players have?** (High barrier, potential moat)

Document each unoccupied position with:
- Position description (what a subject occupying this space would look like)
- Attractiveness rating: Low / Medium / High / Speculative
- Evidence for attractiveness assessment
- Barriers to entry

---

## IDENTIFYING THE STRONGEST DIFFERENTIATOR

Apply a simple scoring pass across all subjects:

| Subject | Distinctiveness | Rarity | Value Creation | Combined Score |
|---------|----------------|--------|----------------|----------------|
| A       | 7              | 6      | 8              | 7.1            |
| B       | 4              | 3      | 5              | 4.0            |
| C       | 9              | 8      | 7              | 8.1            |

**Rank subjects by differentiation strength**, then explain WHY the top-ranked subject is most distinctive — not just that it scored higher, but what specific combination of characteristics produces its differentiation advantage.

---

## SPOTTING SHARED WEAKNESSES

Look for dimensions where ALL or MOST subjects perform poorly. These shared weaknesses reveal:
- **Industry blind spots** — the whole category ignores a user need
- **Conventional assumptions** — everyone does X because everyone does X, not because it's the best approach
- **Innovation opportunities** — the first subject to solve the shared weakness gains category leadership

Document shared weaknesses as: "No subject in this competitive field adequately addresses [dimension]. This represents a potential differentiation opportunity for any player willing to invest in [approach]."

## PRESENTING FINDINGS WITHOUT OVERWHELMING

### Rules for Multi-Competitor Presentation

1. **Lead with the most surprising finding** — not with the most subjects or the longest table
2. **Use progressive disclosure** — executive summary first, full matrix second
3. **Name the biggest takeaway** — "The most differentiated player in this field is C, primarily because..."
4. **Call out the white space** — users want to know where opportunity lives, not just who exists
5. **Limit the full matrix exposure** — if >8 subjects, consider splitting into cluster sub-analyses
6. **Avoid ranking as the main output** — a list of "best to worst" is less useful than a map of "who occupies what space and why"
